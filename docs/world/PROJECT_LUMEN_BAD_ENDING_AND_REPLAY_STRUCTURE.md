# Project Lumen — Bad Ending and Replay Structure

**Status: design proposal only.** This replaces the **bad-ending presentation** in [Endgame Knowledge Reassembly](PROJECT_LUMEN_ENDGAME_KNOWLEDGE_REASSEMBLY.md); it does not change that document's proposed M@P true-route law. It is constrained by [Knowledge Architecture V2](PROJECT_LUMEN_KNOWLEDGE_ARCHITECTURE_V2.md), [Knowledge Discovery Events](PROJECT_LUMEN_KNOWLEDGE_DISCOVERY_EVENTS.md), and the [Spatial Identity Contract](PROJECT_LUMEN_SPATIAL_IDENTITY_CONTRACT.md). No elevator, lower landing, camera, room exchange, ending, replay system, scene, or script is authorized or claimed implemented here. Human comprehension and the lower-site geometry are **UNVALIDATED**.

**Design intent:** the player makes a reasonable, genuine escape attempt using the knowledge that has already worked. A down-only evacuation elevator carries them past four increasingly isolated Atrium-like landings. They escape the immediate experimental interior and reach a safe terminus, but the descent exposes an unresolved pattern: the facility's familiar plan was only one arrangement of connections. The desired feeling is **“I succeeded, but my explanation of what I escaped was incomplete.”** A later playthrough changes the player's attention, not their abilities or the world's rules.

## Contract first: what may actually change?

Under the current quantum law, a bounded object with authored placements can relocate only when its current and candidate participating surfaces are unobserved and the complete move is safe. K6 preserves its identity and attached conditions; K7 proposes **two distinct room shells M and N exchanging fixed P/Q sockets**. Fixed atrium plant, circulation, shaft, socket beds, and exterior ground do not become one moving quantum root. Being inside a cabin does not exempt the passenger's own sight of a participating surface. An elevator motor, floor button, or descent does not cause a quantum transition.

The new pitch has a **literal version that is not currently supported**: one and the same observed Central Atrium loses doors as the elevator descends, or the whole building swaps spatial states because the cabin crosses a floor. That would contradict the fixed-reference and direct-observation contracts and would require a separately specified law, bounded members, candidate placements, observer geometry, safety proof, and human validation. This document does **not** quietly add that rule.

The **recommended version within current scope** distinguishes *experience* from *cause*:

- The evacuation lift travels **physically downward** along a continuous, inspectable shaft. It is ordinary transport and can only be commanded farther down because its return drive/upper brake is visibly damaged. This limitation is a physical condition, not an ending flag or a quantum command.
- Four **different, persistent** landings are built with a shared central-plant architectural grammar but progressively fewer lateral connections. On first encounter they feel like “states” of the familiar Atrium. Their individual repair histories, shaft joints, and depth references prevent the player from being told falsely that the *same* observed room morphed.
- The experience of changing connectivity **echoes K6/K7**—identity, condition, and connection are different questions—but the descent alone proves neither quantum room exchange nor a three-site origin. The player can take the pattern back to the already testable M/N and P/Q evidence on a later run. K8 remains a proposed relationship among sites, not something four similar floors automatically certify.

If the creative requirement is that the **same physical Atrium** undergo four quantum transitions, treat this as a **REVISE / new-law decision before implementation**, not as an alternative interpretation of the existing two-shell proof. Even adding four bounded quantum modules would require identifying their fixed sockets, persistent identities, observer paths, and all legal states; the current M/N pair cannot simply be stretched into a disappearing-door sequence.

## Bad-ending experience

**Player expectation.** After reaching exterior C by a lawful supported A/B/C trip, the player finds an ordinary emergency route to a central evacuation lift. It plausibly serves lower transit or shelter infrastructure. The outside yard remains real; C was not a fake exit. The player can inspect the facility or turn back before choosing descent. The lift's damaged return mechanism is readable before commitment, not disclosed as a punishment afterward.

**Action and emotional progression.** The player descends by choice. The cabin's continuous tracks, changing depth marks, and the unchanged direction of gravity make the motion real. At the first stop, the silhouette of a familiar Atrium and four work approaches offers relief: perhaps this is a lower way out, or perhaps they have looped. At each further stop one or more approaches that *look as if they should be present* meet a complete wall. No wall visibly vanishes while watched. The mechanical journey is stable; the plan is unsettling. At the last stop only a safe central refuge and the shaft-side egress remain—no lateral research approaches. The player survives and can leave the dangerous wing by that ordinary refuge/egress route. The outcome is incomplete understanding, **not** entrapment, death, or a false exit.

**First interpretation.** Repeated construction, evacuation sectors, progressive collapse, or a route that doubles back all plausibly explain the first two landings. A player who already knows the A/B/C ride may suspect a “quantum elevator.” That is a useful *question*, not the answer the ending asserts.

**Final realization.** The same central construction grammar can coexist with different connection patterns. The player is no longer certain that the four approaches of the Atrium defined the whole facility or that a room name identified the same material structure. The landing sequence should make them remember the fixed P scar, M's repaired doorway, N's solid face, Q's different contact, and the camera that seemed merely to record. It should **not** state that these four lower spaces themselves exchanged or that an entire building is quantum. The proven correction remains available through a physical M/N experiment on replay.

**Fairness boundary.** “Bad” is never displayed to the player as a grade. The C escape and the descent's shelter are genuine. The loss is epistemic: the player chose a safe route while an important architectural question remained unresolved. If the final landing becomes an inescapable sealed room, if descent activates a surprise failure flag, or if the first landing is a magical identical copy without physical depth evidence, the outcome will feel like a trap rather than a successful but incomplete journey.

## Four stops: a decreasing connection envelope

The numbers below count **research approaches from each landing's central space**. They are not an instruction that the original upper Atrium loses doors in place. The lift/refuge access is a separate continuous connection and does not count among those four. Each landing has its own unchanging structural wound or service repair, visible at normal eye height, so a careful player can later say “these were different built spaces.” The shared plant form is institutional design, not proof of runtime cloning.

| Stop | Player-facing spatial experience | Plausible first explanation | What persists for a careful comparison | Fragment encountered |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1 — Four approaches** | An Atrium-like central volume with the familiar four-direction plan. | “I descended to another floor of the same facility, perhaps an exit transfer.” | Shaft joint/depth mark and a landing-specific repair; work approaches are actually present. | An ordinary operations fragment counts all four returns against a central reference. |
| **2 — Three approaches** | One expected research mouth is solid, while the other three still function as built approaches. | “That wing was walled off after damage.” | Shaft continues below Stop 1; the central reference repeats as architecture, but its local wear is different. | A service fragment records an intact fixed contact beside the closed frontage. |
| **3 — One approach** | Two more expected mouths are walls; one lateral path remains. | “Lower sectors were deliberately isolated.” | Another distinct repair and intact fixed survey datum make this neither Stop 1 nor Stop 2 revisited. | A monitoring fragment records a live lens's orientation and a mismatched room sighting. |
| **4 — Zero approaches** | Only the central volume, lift threshold, and safe refuge/egress remain; no lateral research route continues. | “This is the last protected level.” | The shaft terminates physically; the central structural motif remains, but its unique damage and footing show a fourth place. | A final survey fragment preserves a comparison of the central footing with the missing side approaches. |

The absence of a route must be material architecture, not a door model that silently toggles when the player is in the lift. A stop with an approach ending in debris differs from a stop whose original wall has no doorway; the latter is the intended uncanny comparison. Distinct repair marks keep the sequence fair rather than a memory test. The player need not enter every surviving branch or collect anything to continue downward. The one-way drive is a visible mechanical limitation, while the safe terminal refuge is an actual outcome.

## Four document fragments: conjunction, not answer key

One short fragment is naturally encountered at each landing's working surface or service threshold. “Receives” means it is placed in the player's path of investigation, not added to a quest inventory or required to start the next descent. A player can ignore one or all four and still reach the terminus. Their text records what a worker observed or assumed; no fragment uses “quantum room,” “spatial state,” “M/N,” or a true-ending instruction. Dates/provenance can give them human context but are not a code to assemble.

| Stop and ordinary source | Example in-world fragment | Prior evidence it reconnects on reflection | What it must not prove by itself |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1: Atrium dispatch sheet**, beside a used route board. | “Four returns walked from the center this shift. The plant notch is still our datum; leave yesterday's route sketch on the board for the next crew.” | The fixed Atrium plant and operations notes that treated an address as a room name. | That every similarly shaped hall is the same room. |
| **2: utility service slip**, clipped at the blank frontage. | “No break in the feed at the side contact. The face beyond it is solid today; do not order another conduit until survey checks the bed.” | Power's fixed contacts versus travelling repairs and an ordinary attached condition (K6). | That a power fault or quantum change caused this landing's wall. |
| **3: monitoring handoff**, beside a visible lens service point. | “Lens remained live after the monitor went dark. The repaired jamb is in the outer view; the older sheet puts that repair on the inner approach.” | Signal's live-camera test, Records' incompatible repair surveys, M's persistent seam at P/Q. | That footage itself observes, or that a camera selects a room. |
| **4: survey margin**, on a battered fixed work surface in the refuge. | “Central footing matches the old measure. None of the side lines can be followed from here. Keep the footing sheet; the address list is not enough.” | The fixed P/Q references and the difference between a coordinate, a shell identity, and a connection (K7). | The historical cause of duplication, K8's three-site geometry, or a specific route to the true ending. |

Together the fragments move from **routine plan → unchanged fixed contact with different face → room-specific repair seen at another approach under a live lens → distrust of address lists**. They do not explain the law; they make earlier, distributed physical observations worth retesting. A player should be able to reach the same suspicion without reading them by noticing shaft continuity, distinct local damage, the fixed references, and the already available M/N evidence. If unbriefed players can solve only by reading all four fragments in order, the evidence architecture has failed.

The fragments are not magically generated by descent. Each belongs to a believable worker and fixed surface at its own landing. If a later design instead puts any paper on a quantum room module, its holder must be authored as an attached member under K6; loose notes cannot inexplicably travel. Do not bundle the four excerpts into a post-ending explanatory montage.

## Spatial logic and the K6/K7 distinction

**Literal motion:** The cabin changes vertical position through a continuous shaft. A one-way motor is a mundane damaged-elevator condition. Seeing the tracks and depth references is important because an unexplained level swap would be read as teleportation or a supernatural loop.

**Experienced “state”:** Each lower landing has a different **configuration of available connections**. The shared Atrium silhouette makes the player compare the first and last. Under the recommended version these are four persistent places, not four mutually exclusive states of one quantum root. The pattern invites the correct question—whether an apparent wall is intrinsic to a place or belongs to whichever bounded structure meets it—without claiming the descent itself demonstrates a quantum transition.

**K6:** The meaningful distinction is that an object's attached repair, door condition, and equipment setting persist with **that object**, while a fixed contact or corridor remains at its position. The lower contact slip can remind the player of this distinction. It would be wrong to say an entire landing's missing approaches are a “persistent state” of the original Atrium merely because the player rode down.

**K7:** The physical proof remains **M and N simultaneously identifiable at P and Q, then exchanging those fixed sockets empty while their histories travel and fixed scars remain**. Four look-alike landings alone are compatible with ordinary repeated construction. A second-run player may use the descent's pattern to notice the upper P frontage earlier, but the pair exchange—not the lift—is what rules out moved furniture, a hidden elevator, or a changing map asset.

**K8:** The descent can suggest a deeper connected site, but its shaft must be placed in a coherent fixed geography before it can support the three-site claim. Four survey notes and a repeated architectural motif cannot prove one persistent spatial history. A future core reference must be physically traceable from known fixed ground and checked against a persistent shell/condition; otherwise the lower sequence remains a thematic echo, not K8 evidence.

This separation protects the player's trust in Awakening. The opening rule stays true; the bad ending exposes an **incomplete scale assumption**, not a new arbitrary law triggered by a lift.

## Replay value: same world, different attention

Replay is a **new traversal with the same rules and evidence**, not New Game+ knowledge points, a retained document counter, altered room behavior, or an unlocked true-ending option. The player may recognize the better route early; the game must allow it. A bad-ending-first order can be emotionally effective but is never mechanically mandatory.

| On the first run | What may stand out on a second run | The physical test now worth making |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Awakening Cube seemed the only abnormal object. | Its observed/unobserved contrast was a trustworthy small case, not a staged trick. | Apply the same hold/release question to a repaired collar and then an apparent wall. |
| The Containment deck looked like a lift. | The collar repair moved while fixed beds stayed; the rider had to avoid observing its own support. | Compare a non-Cube member against a fixed receiver before assuming any larger structure is ordinary scenery. |
| Power's dead contact suggested a faulty machine. | A fixed contact can remain where a moving attached condition no longer is. | Track M's repair and ordinary door/setting against P and Q contacts. |
| Records' plans seemed inconsistent. | Workers may have accurately recorded an **address** while misidentifying the structure occupying it. | Find both M and N from fixed views and compare their different histories before/after an empty exchange. |
| Signal's cameras seemed like security equipment. | A live lens, unlike footage, may have been the missing observer holding a state. | Repeat the same-powered-lens on/off-member test at a known A/B/C member, then predict a P/Q hold. |
| The four descending halls looked like the facility looping. | Shaft depth and individual repairs show different physical spaces; the repeated plan draws attention to connections, not magical repetition. | Return to the **upper** fixed Atrium and ask whether its P face belongs to the socket or to a persistent room shell. |

Previously read documents can also change meaning without changing text: an address-based dispatch sheet becomes an honest source of confusion; a supply complaint becomes incomplete when a lens was watching; a repair note identifies a *material structure* rather than a coordinate. The four lower fragments should point back to these scattered clues, not become a set of pages the player must memorize. The second run's strongest moment is a **before-action prediction** about M/N at P/Q and the camera's actual view, followed by a lawful empty exchange.

## True-ending contrast and replay decision

The player who follows C and the down-only lift has correctly applied K1/K3/K4 and found a real evacuation path. The decreasing approaches make them question the model “the facility is a stable container with one abnormal machine.” They may survive with that question unresolved. This is the proposed Bad Ending's sting, not an accusation.

The player who investigates further distinguishes M and N from P and Q, sees that M's ordinary doorway and attached state persist (K6), proves an empty reciprocal exchange (K7), and understands that a live camera may hold the current arrangement (K5). Removing that actual view and all other relevant observation can **permit**, not select, M@P. M's existing doorway can then meet a fixed exterior spur. K2 applies wherever a candidate is genuinely observed; K4's C trip is not the final proof; K8 may deepen the site's history but cannot be a hidden route gate. The true outcome follows a corrected spatial prediction, not four collected fragments or the fact that the player has already seen the Bad Ending.

The revised Bad Ending must not replace the earlier true-route setup with a “correct floor” choice. The contrast is **ordinary evacuation through known apparatus** versus **understanding which architectural object can occupy a fixed connection**. Both are lawful actions. If the elevator becomes the only way to learn a required camera or room rule, the true route has become bad-ending-gated and must be revised.

## Decision gates before committing to this ending

1. **Canon choice:** Approve the recommended ordinary downward landings as an experiential pattern, or explicitly reopen the spatial law if literal quantum transitions of the same Atrium are required. Do not describe both as the same thing.
2. **Physical fairness:** Draw the continuous shaft, four distinct landings and their unique repairs, each missing approach's construction, the lift's one-way failure, and a real safe terminal refuge/egress. Verify the C yard remains a genuine exterior vantage and the descent is voluntary before its irreversible section.
3. **Evidence balance:** Verify that each fragment has a plausible owner and location, is short enough to be read as lived-in residue, and is unnecessary for the true-route causal proof. The lower sequence must not be the first or only sight of a camera, fixed contact, M/N repair, or connection mismatch.
4. **No loop confusion:** In unbriefed tests, ask after Stop 2 whether players think they returned to the same Atrium, reached another built space, or saw a transformation—and what visible fact would distinguish those. If every player reports a supernatural loop, strengthen shaft/depth and local identity cues, not an explanatory caption.
5. **Replay payoff:** After an unbriefed second run, ask what the player predicted about the P/Q pair, camera, and exterior spur *before* acting. If they only report “I collected four hints and found the good elevator,” the ending has failed the knowledge-progression goal.

**Current recommendation:** retain the new descent as a genuine, unsettling **ordinary evacuation path through four distinct fixed landings**, with progressive loss of connections as an experiential pattern. Do **not** claim the lift itself performs quantum facility-state transitions under the present law. Literal state-changing architecture is a separate unresolved design revision, not an implementation detail to hide inside the Bad Ending.
